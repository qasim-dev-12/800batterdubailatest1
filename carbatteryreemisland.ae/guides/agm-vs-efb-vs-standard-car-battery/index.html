AGM vs EFB vs Standard Battery: Which Does Your Car Need?

You have seen AGM or EFB on a quote or a battery label and want to know whether it matters. It does. Here is how each type is built, which cars need which, and how to read the label so you buy the right one.

Most drivers only think about battery type when a quote arrives with "AGM" on it and a higher price than they expected. The natural question is whether that letters-and-numbers code is a real requirement or an upsell. For a lot of modern cars, it is a real requirement.

There are three main types of 12V lead-acid starter battery in use today: the standard flooded battery, the enhanced flooded battery (EFB) and the absorbent glass mat battery (AGM). They all do the same basic job of starting the engine and supporting the electrics, but they are built differently and they cope with very different workloads.

This guide explains what each one is, why stop-start and regenerative braking changed the picture, what happens if you fit the wrong type, and how to read the label on your current battery so you can match it.

What a standard flooded battery is

A standard flooded battery, sometimes called a wet or conventional battery, has lead plates sitting in liquid electrolyte (a mix of sulphuric acid and water). Most are sold as maintenance-free, meaning the case is sealed for everyday purposes and you do not top it up.

Flooded batteries are good at what older cars asked of them: one big burst of current to crank the engine, then a long recharge from the alternator while you drive. They are not designed to be partly drained and recharged over and over through the day. Repeated deep discharge wears the plates and shortens their life.

They remain the right choice for many cars without stop-start, and they are usually the cheapest option.

What EFB means

EFB stands for enhanced flooded battery. It is still a flooded battery with liquid electrolyte, but it is built to handle far more charge and discharge cycles than a standard one.

The main changes are inside: carbon additives in the plate material improve how quickly the battery accepts charge, and a polyester scrim (a fine mesh layer) on the plates helps hold the active material in place so it does not shed as quickly under repeated cycling.

EFB batteries are commonly fitted to entry-level stop-start cars, where the engine cuts out at traffic lights and the battery has to run the electrics and then restart the engine many times on a single journey.

AGM battery meaning

AGM stands for absorbent glass mat. Instead of free liquid, the electrolyte is soaked into fine glass-fibre mats packed tightly between the plates. The battery is valve regulated, so it is sealed and recombines most of the gas it produces internally rather than venting it.

That construction gives AGM batteries the best deep-cycle performance and the best charge acceptance of the three types. In plain terms, an AGM battery copes best with being drained and recharged repeatedly, and it takes charge back quickly when the alternator has a chance to supply it.

This is why AGM is the usual fitment on cars with more advanced stop-start systems, regenerative braking and heavy electrical loads. Many BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Audi, Volkswagen and Range Rover models leave the factory with an AGM battery, though the exact fitment depends on the model and engine.

Bosch, Varta and Exide all sell AGM and EFB ranges. The brand matters less than fitting the type and size your car was designed for.

Why stop-start and regenerative braking need EFB or AGM

In a stop-start car, every time the engine switches off in traffic, the battery alone runs the air conditioning fan, lights, screens and control units. Then it has to restart the engine. On a busy Dubai commute that can happen dozens of times per trip, which is a cycling workload a standard battery was never built for.

Regenerative braking adds a second demand. When you lift off or brake, the car uses the alternator to recover energy and push it back into the battery in short, strong bursts. The battery needs high charge acceptance to soak that up, and AGM handles it best.

The car's battery management system is set up around the type of battery it expects. It decides when stop-start is allowed and how hard to charge, based on that type.

What happens if you downgrade a stop-start car

Fitting a standard flooded battery to a car designed for EFB or AGM is the most common mistake, usually because it is cheaper on the day. It tends to cost more later.

  • Early failure: the flooded battery is cycled far harder than it was built for and can wear out well before its normal life.
  • Stop-start faults: the battery management system may see low charge and disable stop-start, or show a battery or stop-start warning.
  • Charging mismatch: the car may charge the battery using a profile meant for a different type, which can overcharge or undercharge it.
  • Electrical niggles: weak voltage during restarts can cause screens to reboot or warning lights to flicker.

When an upgrade makes sense

The general rule is that upgrading is acceptable and downgrading is not. Moving from flooded to EFB, or from EFB to AGM, is generally fine as long as the size, terminal layout and capacity suit the car.

An upgrade can make sense if your car does many short trips, sits parked for long spells in a basement bay, runs a dashcam or other accessories, or has gone through flooded batteries quickly. It costs more up front, so it is worth it when the car's use is hard on batteries, not by default.

On cars with battery management, an upgrade from one type to another should also be registered or coded so the car charges the new battery correctly.

How to read a car battery label

Before you buy, look at the label on the battery already in the car. It tells you most of what you need to match.

  • Type: look for "AGM", "EFB" or "Start-Stop" printed on the label. If you see one of these, do not go back to a standard battery.
  • Ah (ampere-hours): the capacity, shown as a number such as 70Ah or 80Ah. Stay at or close to the original figure.
  • CCA (cold cranking amps): how much current the battery can deliver to start the engine. Match or exceed the original.
  • Terminal layout: which side the positive terminal sits on. A battery with the terminals the other way round may not fit the cables.
  • Case size and hold-down: the physical dimensions and the base ledge that the clamp grips. The battery has to sit securely.
  • Date code: a code showing when the battery was made. Formats vary by brand, so ask if you are unsure.

Registration and coding on European cars

Many BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Audi, Volkswagen and Land Rover models use an intelligent battery sensor on the negative terminal. The car tracks the battery's age, state of charge and health and adjusts charging to suit. When a new battery is fitted, the car should be told, a step usually called registration or coding.

Without it, the car may keep charging the new battery as if it were the old, worn one. Registration matters most when the capacity or type changes, for example moving from 80Ah to 90Ah or from EFB to AGM, because the car then needs the new details.

Questions

What does AGM mean on a car battery?

AGM stands for absorbent glass mat. The electrolyte is held in glass-fibre mats between the plates rather than as free liquid, and the battery is sealed and valve regulated. It handles repeated charging and discharging best, which suits stop-start cars.

Can I put a normal battery in a car that came with AGM?

It is not recommended. A standard flooded battery is likely to fail early in a car built for AGM and can cause stop-start faults and warning lights. Stay with AGM, or match whatever type the car was designed for.

Is EFB the same as AGM?

No. EFB is an improved flooded battery with liquid electrolyte, built to cope with more cycling than a standard battery. AGM uses glass mats to hold the electrolyte and performs better again for deep cycling and charge acceptance. A car designed for AGM should not be given an EFB.

Can I upgrade from a standard battery to AGM?

Generally yes, as long as the size, terminal layout and capacity suit the car. It costs more, so it is most worthwhile for cars that do short trips, sit parked for long periods or run extra accessories. On cars with battery management, the change should be registered.

How do I know which battery type my car needs?

Check the label on the current battery for AGM, EFB or Start-Stop, and check the handbook. If the car has stop-start, it almost certainly needs EFB or AGM. A technician can confirm the right type, size and capacity before fitting.
