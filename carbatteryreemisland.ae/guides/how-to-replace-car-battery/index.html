How to Replace a Car Battery Safely, and When to Leave It to a Technician

Replacing a car battery is a straightforward job on many cars, as long as you follow the right order and fit the right battery. This guide covers the tools, the steps, how to match the replacement, and the situations where a DIY swap tends to cause trouble.

On a simple car with the battery under the bonnet, a battery change takes a careful owner around half an hour. The steps are not complicated, but the order matters, and a few mistakes, such as removing the wrong terminal first or fitting a battery that does not match, can cause sparks, electrical faults or a new battery that fails early.

Many modern cars also make the job harder than it looks. Stop-start systems, battery registration on European cars, and batteries hidden in the boot or under a seat all add steps that the usual online instructions skip.

This guide gives honest steps for a standard battery change, explains how to choose the right replacement, and lists the situations where it is usually better to call a technician. If you are unsure at any point, stop and get help: a battery stores enough energy to cause burns and damage if it is shorted.

Safety first

Car batteries contain sulphuric acid and can give off hydrogen gas, particularly while charging. Treat them with care:

  • Wear eye protection and gloves. Acid on skin or in eyes needs immediate rinsing with plenty of clean water.
  • Keep sparks, flames and cigarettes away from the battery.
  • Remove rings, watches and metal bracelets. A spanner across both terminals, or between the positive terminal and the car body, can cause a short circuit.
  • Work with the engine off, the key out and the car in park with the handbrake on.
  • Lift with your legs. A car battery is heavy, often 15 kg or more.
  • Do not tip the battery or work on one with a cracked or bulging case.

Most battery changes need only basic tools, but check your car first: some hold-downs use extension bars or unusual bolt sizes.

Match the replacement battery before you start

The most common DIY mistake is buying a battery that is close but not right. Check the old battery's label and your owner's manual, and match all of the following:

  • Type: flooded, EFB or AGM. A car that left the factory with EFB or AGM, usually because of stop-start, needs the same type or better.
  • Capacity (Ah): the amp-hour rating should match the original.
  • Cold cranking amps (CCA): match or exceed the original figure.
  • Case size: the battery must sit in the tray and fit under the hold-down without moving.
  • Terminal layout: positive on the correct side, and the right post type. Reversed terminals mean the cables may not reach, or may be stretched against metal.

Our guide to AGM, EFB and standard batteries explains how to read the label and which type your car needs.

Step by step: how to change a car battery

These steps cover a typical car with the battery under the bonnet. Follow your owner's manual where it differs.

  • 1. Switch everything off, remove the key and make sure you know any radio or alarm codes.
  • 2. Connect a memory saver if you have one, following its instructions, to keep settings and avoid resets.
  • 3. Disconnect the negative (–) terminal first. It is usually black and marked with a minus sign. Tuck the cable away so it cannot spring back onto the post.
  • 4. Disconnect the positive (+) terminal. Lift any cover first and keep tools clear of the car body.
  • 5. Remove the hold-down clamp or bracket, and any vent tube on batteries that have one.
  • 6. Lift the old battery straight out, keeping it upright.
  • 7. Clean the tray, then clean the terminal clamps with a brush until they are bright metal. Neutralise any acid residue.
  • 8. Place the new battery in the tray the right way round, with the terminals on the correct sides, and refit the hold-down so the battery cannot move.
  • 9. Connect the positive (+) terminal first and tighten it firmly.
  • 10. Connect the negative (–) terminal last and tighten it. A small spark at this point is normal; a large one means something is still drawing power.
  • 11. Remove the memory saver, apply terminal protector, start the car and check that the warning lights clear.

The rule to remember: negative off first, negative on last. It keeps the spanner from shorting the positive terminal against the car body.

After fitting: checks worth doing

Once the car starts, check that the battery warning light is off and the engine idles normally. Some cars need the windows' one-touch function, the steering angle sensor or the clock reset; the owner's manual explains how.

If you have a multimeter, measure the voltage across the terminals with the engine running. A reading clearly lower than the battery's resting voltage, or one that climbs very high, suggests a charging system fault that will shorten the new battery's life.

When DIY causes problems

On some cars a battery change is no longer a simple swap. These are the situations where owners most often run into trouble:

  • Battery registration: many BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Audi, Volkswagen and Range Rover models need the new battery registered with the car's battery management system. Without it, the car can charge the new battery as if it were the old one, leading to early failure or warning lights.
  • AGM and EFB batteries in stop-start cars: fitting the wrong type, or not resetting the system, can lead to stop-start faults and early failure.
  • Batteries in the boot or under a seat: these are often vented through a tube and may sit beside trim, wiring and fuel lines, so access and refitting take more care.
  • Auxiliary batteries: some premium cars have a second battery that also needs checking.
  • Basement car parks: some buildings restrict repairs in parking bays, and a dropped battery on a basement floor is an acid spill in a shared space. Check your building's rules first.
  • Disposal: an old car battery must be recycled, not put in household bins. It contains lead and acid that are harmful if dumped.

Questions

Which terminal do I disconnect first when changing a car battery?

Disconnect the negative (–) terminal first and reconnect it last. This stops a tool touching the car body from shorting the positive terminal.

Will I lose my car's settings when I change the battery?

You may lose radio presets, the clock, window settings and some learned values. A memory saver keeps power to the car while the battery is out and avoids most of these resets.

Can I fit a standard battery in place of an AGM or EFB battery?

It is not recommended. A stop-start car designed for EFB or AGM needs the same type or better, or the battery is likely to fail early and stop-start may not work properly.

Do I need to register a new battery on my BMW or Mercedes?

Many models do need registration so the car charges the new battery correctly. Check your model or ask a technician with the right diagnostic equipment.

What should I do with the old battery?

Take it to a recycling point or hand it back to the seller. Car batteries contain lead and acid and must not go in household waste. We take old batteries away for recycling when we fit or deliver one.
